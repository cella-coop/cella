It's amazing to see how - since the very begining of democratic revival in the late 18th century - society has mobilized to enable innovations in direct democracy. We have seen the emergence of new institutional and organizational models, facilitation processes, and - more recently - open-source tools. We have a thriving ecosystem of options. But we have to stop and ask ourselves: why have these fantastic innovations seen such limited adoption, beyond specific niches?

Our social and technological systems are complex enough to preclude a simple answer. There are, in all likelihood, many factors. But I want to make the case that the bottleneck is this: democracy is often complex and slow, and operations need to be simple and quick for an organization or institution to be effective. And of course, effective organizations and institutions usually out-compete ineffective ones.

Ancient direct democracy and its scale (small). Dunbar's number etc. Emergence of modern democracy changed the scale, and emergence of increasingly rapid and globalized communication have created a variety of problems.

Within organizations, democracy struggles to scale in both headcount and speed.

To make things worse, as we strive to make deliberation more inclusive and fair, it because bureacratic, slow and frustrating. And as Jo Freeman noted in the Tyranny of Structurelessness, this very effort can result in invisible and insiduous power structures.

What we need: democratic tools that operate well accross scales, can operate quickly as well as slowly (to meet the needs of the community and occassion).

To do this, I believe we need modern appropriate technology. I also believe our communities deserve high quality design, on a human scale.

To achieve this, one needs a financially viable and autonomous organization. As far as I can see, the only reliable approach to achieving this is building a business. And of course, for lovers of democracy, this business must be a cooperative.
