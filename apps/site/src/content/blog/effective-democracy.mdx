It's amazing to see how - since the very begining of democratic revival in the late 18th century - society has mobilized to enable innovations in direct democracy. We have seen the emergence of new institutional and organizational models, facilitation processes, and - more recently - open-source tools. We have a thriving ecosystem of options. But we have to stop and ask ourselves: why have these fantastic innovations seen such limited adoption, beyond specific niches?

Our social and technological systems are complex enough to preclude a simple answer. There are, in all likelihood, many
factors. But I want to make the case that the bottleneck is this: democracy is often complex and slow, but operations
need to be simple and quick for an organization or institution to be effective. And of course, effective organizations
and institutions usually out-compete ineffective ones.

<Band ink="success" icon="bank" heading="effective democratic organizations" quote>
  **Democracy is often complex and slow**, but **operations need to be simple and quick** for an organization or
  institution to be effective.
</Band>

For hundreds of years, cooperatives have effectively operated using democratic principles. Traditionally, a coop's _general assembly_ sets broad policies and elects a _governing council_. This means coop worker-owners have self determination, more equitable profit sharing and greater job security than regular workers. But operationally, the governing council often creates management structures similar to those in corporate environments.

Now, this _has_ proven to be an effective way to run a democratic organizations. There are a great many shining examples of cooperatives around the world that compete in a global market against investor-owned companies.
